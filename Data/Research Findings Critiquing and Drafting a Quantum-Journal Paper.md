# Research Findings: Critiquing and Drafting a Quantum-Journal Paper that Combines Formal Theory with Empirical Ablation (WQPE / Modular Shor)

## TL;DR
- **Quantum is highly selective and cares most about (1) a clearly stated contributions summary in the first two pages, (2) honest labeling of what is proven vs. heuristic, and (3) reproducibility of empirical claims — the exact three axes your compiled report needs to nail.** Editors read only the first few pages (and no proofs) before deciding suitability, so front-load the contributions list and defer proofs to appendices.
- **The dominant failure mode reviewers punish in theory+empirical papers is a mismatch between claim scope and evidence scope** (overclaiming from small-N ablations, unlabeled heuristic claims, unjustified statistical-test choices). Lead every empirical claim with an effect size + confidence interval, not a p-value; state the McNemar variant and bootstrap resample count; and put the 144-trial ablation's scope limits in the main text, not buried.
- **The self-caught audit-script bug (55.4%→0% false-accept) is not an erratum and should not be labeled one** — caught pre-publication, it is simply transparent methods reporting. Narrate it matter-of-factly in the main text where the audit is described, with a quantitative before→after in an appendix and a code link; no apology. This *builds* reviewer trust rather than undermining it, and empirical evidence (below) shows self-correction raises perceived trustworthiness.

## Key Findings

### 1. Reviewer expectations and common weaknesses (Quantum, ACM TQC)

**Quantum's acceptance bar and reviewer form.** Quantum is explicitly "a highly selective journal. Correct but incremental work is below threshold. A very significant technical or conceptual contribution is necessary for acceptance." Original-research acceptance criteria are: significantly advances the sub-field; contains a very significant technical or conceptual contribution; and "provides evidence that the employed methods or obtained results go significantly beyond the state of the art." Quantum's Editorial policies page states verbatim: "Rejection rate: No target rejection rate. Quantum does not aim for a pre-set number of published papers per year and there is no target rejection rate. The quality of every paper is evaluated independently of other submissions." The realistic acceptance picture: Quantum's own January-2019 author-feedback post, of "222 papers that have reached a final decision," states "The actual acceptance rate for Quantum is closer to 50%, so this sample has a strong acceptance bias" — but note wide editor-level variance (one Quantum handling editor reported a 19% personal acceptance rate over 42 handled submissions). Most rejections happen at the editor pre-screen stage before external review, and these are unappealable.

The referee form asks reviewers a fixed set of questions that map directly onto what you must nail in the draft:
- "Summary: what are the main questions posed by the manuscript and how does it answer them?"
- "To what extent have you checked the technical correctness of the paper?"
- "Comment on the presentation... Are the main results clearly laid out? **Does the manuscript clearly describe assumptions and limitations?** Is the literature review adequate?"
- "If the submission includes numerical or physical experiments, does it provide sufficient details such that they could be reproduced by readers? This includes for example source code, documentation, experimental data, experimental setup specifications, etc."

Two structural consequences: (a) an explicit "assumptions and limitations" description is a scored line item, not optional; (b) reproducibility of the ~1,350-trial campaign and the 144-trial ablation is a scored line item — release code/data under a FOSS license, which per Quantum's author instructions "will be taken into account positively when making editorial decisions."

**Editor first-read behavior.** Quantum's editors "are not expected to read through proofs at this point, just the main text and statements." For long papers "editors are only required to read the first few pages at this stage." Quantum's author instructions require the main results and assumptions to be stated "in the first couple of pages... for example, in a 'Contributions' subsection in the Introduction," and say to aim that summary at "the 2-3 editors closest to the topic." This is the single highest-leverage structural fix for a 1000-line compiled report.

**ACM TQC** publishes "high-impact, original research," desk-rejects on scope/novelty/quality/formatting, requires three qualified reviews for any acceptance, uses the ACM template, and asks reviewers to be specific and refer to page numbers, avoid value judgments, and give constructive criticism. Accepted contributions "are expected to be highly impactful within their sub-domain." TQC explicitly welcomes classical-design-methods-for-quantum work (it ran a special issue, "Using Classical Design Methods to Accelerate Progress in Quantum Computing"), which is a good fit for a classical post-processing contribution. Its scope names "quantum algorithms and complexity" and "NISQ implementation" directly.

**Genre-specific weaknesses reviewers flag** (synthesized from PRX Quantum referee guidance, the ECE/CS statistical-evaluation playbook, and quantum-methodology critiques):
- **Overclaiming / results "oversold."** PRX Quantum instructs referees: "Are the main conclusions or claims well supported? In case you think that the results are oversold, please explain in detail." A recent systematic review of 81 quantum-error-mitigation papers (arXiv:2605.29872) found weak statistical reporting is endemic, using a checklist including "Neg. Results: Failure cases or limitations reported?" and "Reproducibility: Code, data, or sufficient detail?" This is the standard against which your empirical campaign will be judged.
- **Small-N / single-regime generalization.** The "quantum-industrial complex" critique (arXiv:2511.15642) names "overstated algorithmic speedup claims, selective benchmark reporting, inadequate disclosure of experimental limitations." Your 100% success and p=3.4e-21 headline will draw scrutiny precisely because a perfect score from a 144-trial study invites the "is this cherry-picked to the regime?" objection.
- **Heuristic-vs-proven conflation.** See §5 below — reviewers in mathematically-inclined venues expect strict reservation of "theorem/proposition" for rigorously proven statements.
- **Weak related-work positioning** — failing to discuss closely related work "can lead to rejection."
- **Verbose/redundant proofs in main text** crowding out the contribution; the fix is proof sketches in-text, full proofs in appendix (§2).

### 2. Structuring a paper that mixes formal propositions/proofs with empirical validation

**Where proofs go.** The strong cross-venue convention (NeurIPS checklist, echoed across hundreds of papers) is: **state theorems + full assumptions in the main text; put complete proofs in an appendix; include a short proof sketch in the main text to convey intuition.** The NeurIPS theory guideline: "The proofs can either appear in the main paper or the supplemental material, but if they appear in the supplemental material, the authors are encouraged to provide a short proof sketch to provide intuition. Inversely, any informal proof provided in the core of the paper should be complemented by formal proofs provided in appendix." LaTeX tooling (`apxproof`, `proof-at-the-end`) automates deferring proofs while keeping theorem restatements in the appendix. For your six results (symmetry, monotonicity, asymptotic consistency, conservativity/baseline-reproduction, minimal-prefix-correctness, exact carry-check classifier lemma): keep statements + one- to two-sentence intuition inline; move full proofs to a clearly sectioned appendix that restates each result.

**Which proofs deserve main-text detail.** Because Quantum editors don't read proofs at screening, main-text proof real estate should be spent only on the one or two results that are genuinely load-bearing for the contribution narrative (e.g., the exact carry-check classifier lemma if it underpins correctness, and the conservativity/baseline-reproduction proposition that justifies "safe by default"). Everything else: statement + sketch inline, proof in appendix.

**Abstract.** The proven structure for an algorithms paper (Fournier-Viger; Stanford's Widom, "Tips for Writing Technical Papers"; approximatelycorrect ML-writing heuristics): (1) problem + why it matters, (2) gap/limitation of current state, (3) what you propose, (4) key features of the solution, (5) one or two sentences of results/implications. Use present tense for contributions, past tense for experiments. "Go big: clearly state your major contribution (can also lead with this)." Keep it short.

**Introduction + contributions list.** End the introduction with an explicit "Summary of Contributions" bullet list that doubles as a paper outline, "mentioning in which sections they can be found" (Widom). This satisfies both Quantum's first-two-pages requirement and the NeurIPS "claims must match results" norm. Each bullet should map to a proposition/figure/table. A model justification pattern from a published NeurIPS-checklist answer: "Each claim in the abstract and introduction maps directly to a proposition, theorem, corollary, and/or figure containing empirical data."

**Novelty vs. related work.** Best practice for a classical-post-processing contribution positioned against prior Bayesian/ML/DP/beam-search/graph-search reconstruction:
- Thematic (not chronological) organization; each paragraph summarizes a cluster of prior work, and a **final paragraph states your positioning and the gap you fill** — do not sprinkle your contribution through every paragraph.
- "It is not good enough to be different, your work must be better in some way" — for each prior method class, state concretely what it does well and the specific gap (e.g., beam search lacks a confidence-calibrated stopping rule; Bayesian QAE/phase methods optimize quantum queries but not classical reconstruction cost; DP/graph-search give exactness but no adaptive resource-starvation robustness).
- Avoid both overclaiming ("to the best of our knowledge" as a hedge is acceptable) and underselling. Distinguish a **Background** section (explains QPE/windowed-circuit primitives the reader needs) from a **Related Work** section (positions against competitors). Related work can go after the introduction (if novelty might be questioned early) or before the conclusion (if the need is uncontested).

**Body ordering for theory+empirical.** A workable skeleton: Introduction (+contributions) → Background (standard QPE, windowed reproduction) → Formal model (C_w statistic; modules B/C/D) with inline proposition statements + sketches → Complexity analysis → Empirical methodology (failure-mode campaign design, ablation design, statistical methods) → Results (ablation, calibration) → Limitations → Conclusion → Appendices (full proofs; full sweep tables; reproducibility/correction note).

### 3. Presenting statistical hypothesis testing (McNemar, bootstrap, Holm–Bonferroni)

**Lead with effect size, not p.** The ECE/CS statistical-evaluation playbook (arXiv:2605.00428, Krishnamachari, USC) is the single best template. Its mandated reporting order for any headline comparison: (1) absolute effect in natural units, (2) relative effect, (3) confidence interval on the difference, (4) regime where the claim holds, (5) test result "last and shortest." Rationale: "A small p-value tells you the difference is unlikely under the null hypothesis. It does not tell you the difference is large. With enough seeds, any non-zero difference becomes 'significant.'" So report your 52.1%→100% as absolute (+47.9 pp) and relative gains with a bootstrap CI on the paired difference *first*, then McNemar p=3.4e-21.

**McNemar specifics reviewers expect.** Because your outcomes are paired binary (baseline vs. adaptive on the same trials), McNemar is the correct choice — and this is defensible by citing Thomas G. Dietterich, "Approximate Statistical Tests for Comparing Supervised Classification Learning Algorithms," *Neural Computation* 10(7):1895–1923 (1998), which establishes that "For algorithms that can be executed only once, McNemar's test is the only test with acceptable type I error," while the naive difference-of-two-proportions test has "high probability of type I error... and should never be used." You must specify the variant: **state explicitly whether you used the chi-square approximation with/without continuity correction or the exact binomial version.** The exact binomial form is required when discordant pairs are few; as one exemplar states, "The exact form is required because several discordant counts in our study fall below the b+c ≳ 25 threshold for the standard chi-square approximation." Report the discordant counts (b, c) themselves and an effect-size companion (odds ratio, the raw difference in error proportions, or the phi coefficient, a common McNemar effect size). Note also the Dietterich limitation: McNemar captures the relative difference in error proportion on one test set, not training-set/run-to-run variability — worth a one-line caveat.

**Bootstrap CI specifics.** Report: resample count (B; commonly 1,000–10,000; Efron & Tibshirani suggest 1,000–2,000 for 90–95% CIs, but B=10,000 gives more tail-stable CIs), the CI type (percentile vs. BCa), the resampling unit (trials/seeds — must match the unit of analysis, avoiding pseudoreplication), whether the bootstrap is paired (bootstrap per-pair differences, not the two arms separately), and a fixed seed for reproducibility. Exemplar phrasing: "All confidence intervals reported in the paper are 95% percentile bootstrap intervals with n=1000 resamples and fixed seed 42... For paired comparisons, bootstrap the per-pair differences." State that the point estimate is computed once on the full sample, with CI bounds from the 2.5/97.5 percentiles.

**Multiple-comparison correction.** If you run a family of comparisons (multiple modules/ablation cells), pre-declare the primary comparison and apply Holm–Bonferroni to the family. Holm's method (Holm, S. (1979), "A simple sequentially rejective multiple test procedure," *Scandinavian Journal of Statistics* 6(2):65–70) belongs to the family of closed testing procedures and is uniformly more powerful than Bonferroni — every rejection Bonferroni makes, Holm also makes, plus potentially more — while still controlling the family-wise error rate and requiring no independence assumption. Report transparently: the family size and how it was defined, a table of original p-values sorted ascending with the Holm sequential thresholds and reject/retain decisions, and how many survive. Consider reporting Benjamini–Hochberg (FDR) as a secondary/robustness check if the family is large. Exemplar significance-marker convention: "** p<0.01, * p<0.05, † p<0.10."

**Table format.** One row per comparison; columns for n, discordant (b,c), absolute effect + 95% CI, exact p, Holm-adjusted threshold/decision. Put per-comparison methodology (McNemar variant, B, seed, resampling unit) in a "Statistical methods" subsection or an appendix "Statistical robustness" section; keep the main results table clean with a caption footnote pointing to it. Reviewers expect the *choice justification* (why McNemar, why exact, why Holm) in the main text and the mechanical detail deferrable to appendix.

### 4. Writing the "Limitations" section

**Norms.** NeurIPS is the cross-disciplinary reference standard and is unambiguous: "authors should be rewarded rather than punished for being up front about the limitations of their work." A dedicated "Limitations" section is encouraged; "Point out any strong assumptions and how robust your results are to violations of these assumptions (e.g., independence assumptions, noiseless settings... asymptotic approximations only holding locally). Reflect on the scope of your claims, e.g., if you only tested your approach on a few datasets or did a few runs." Quantum's referee form independently scores whether "the manuscript clearly describe[s] assumptions and limitations."

**Placement.** Limitations belong in the **main text as a dedicated section**, not buried in a discussion paragraph or an appendix. Burying limitations reads as "naïve or defensive" to reviewers; upfront limitations "strengthen the manuscript" and "demonstrate scientific integrity." For your paper the load-bearing limitations to state plainly: (a) empirical scope — small-N regime, specific composite sizes, the compilation-cliff scaling boundary; (b) the raw C_w overconfidence (Brier 0.2239) and that isotonic recalibration (Brier 0.1988) is not wired into the shipped decision rule by default; (c) simulation-only vs. hardware; (d) which properties are proven vs. empirically observed.

**Framing.** Neutral, professional, constructive — "acknowledge the limitation calmly, explain its implications and then indicate why the study remains valuable in spite of it." Pair each limitation with either a mitigation, a scoping statement, or a future-work pointer. Do not use limitations to pre-emptively argue with reviewers. Ensure consistency: the limitations must match what the abstract/results claim (the calibration limitation in particular must be reflected wherever C_w confidence is discussed).

### 5. Disclosing the self-identified correction (55.4%→0% false-accept bug)

**Core reframing: this is NOT an erratum/corrigendum.** COPE and ICMJE correction machinery governs the *published Version of Record*. ICMJE: "Corrections are needed for errors of fact that should have been recognized at the time of publication." COPE distinguishes erratum (journal/production error) from corrigendum (author error) — both post-publication. Because your bug was caught and fixed *before* submission, there is **no formal-notice obligation**; it is simply transparent methods reporting. Do not label it "Erratum" or "Correction" (those labels would be inaccurate and needlessly alarm-signaling). Precedent that in-review code-bug fixes are handled this way: in Jäger et al. (2020), a reviewer found a code error during review and "the error was corrected in the revision" — no erratum (documented in the *Journal of Memory & Language* meta-study "Share the code, not just the data").

**Structural exemplars (how others did it inline):**
- arXiv:2011.06244 ("A Fine-grained Data Set and Analysis of Tangling in Bug Fixing Commits") added an inline numbered Methods subsection, "3.6.3 Data Correction Phase," narrating a bug found in their own data-collection software, quantifying impact (~10% of data), describing remediation, and explicitly stating "The changes to the results were very minor and the analysis of the results was not affected... we... found that there were no relevant changes." This is the closest structural model.
- arXiv:2210.10266 ("Corrected Evaluation Results of the NTCIR WWW-2/3/4 English Subtasks") maps "this table replaces Table X" and states explicitly which measures "are not affected by the bug."
- Cautionary counter-example: the Mann et al. PLOS ONE animal-groups paper had a Matlab down-sampling bug found *after* publication, forcing a Correction and then a full Retraction — the cost of not catching it pre-publication.

**Recommended placement (synthesized):**
1. **Primary: a brief, matter-of-fact statement in the Methods/audit section exactly where the carry-check audit is described** — state the bug, when found, what it affected, the corrected result (55.4%→0%), and crucially a sentence confirming the corrected pipeline was validated. This is the highest-trust location because reviewers can evaluate the fix in context.
2. **Supporting: an appendix "reproducibility note"** showing before→after quantitatively with a link to the fixed code/commit. This mirrors ICMJE's endorsement that, for honest errors leading to a major change in results, "it is helpful to show the extent of the changes in supplementary material or in an appendix, for complete transparency."
3. **Avoid a footnote for the headline number** — burying a reversed headline finding in a footnote risks looking evasive and creates a text/results inconsistency reviewers will flag.
4. **Tone:** constructive, no apology — COPE's case guidance "Corrigendum or erratum?" states verbatim: "An apology by the authors is not needed." (COPE's Retraction Guidelines similarly note "The purpose of retraction is to correct the literature and ensure its integrity, not to punish the authors.") Ensure every downstream section reports only the corrected 0% figure.

**Why this helps rather than hurts.** The preregistered peer-reviewed studies by Altenmüller, Nuding & Gollwitzer (2021, *Public Understanding of Science*, DOI 10.1177/09636625211022181; overall N = 702) found that "such self-correction (compared to no self-correction) increases perceivers' epistemic trustworthiness ascriptions, credibility perceptions, and willingness to further engage with science... researchers' fear that self-criticism... may damage their reputation may be unfounded." Open code is what converts an error disclosure from a liability into a credibility asset. This is a distinctive strength you should use, not hide — it is direct evidence of the audit rigor that Quantum's technical-correctness reviewers reward.

### 6. Quantum journal specifics and structural exemplars

**Length / "quality over quantity."** Quantum has "no typesetting, format or length constraints" but requires a non-technical contributions summary up front. There is no target rejection rate (see the verbatim policy quoted in §1), and editors weight impact/seniority explicitly *out*: "The expected impact... and the perceived seniority of the authors are not acceptance criteria." Practical implication for a 1000-line report: length is allowed, but because editors read only the first pages, ruthless front-loading matters more than trimming; move the exhaustive sweep tables to appendices.

**Proofs vs. empirical weighting for an algorithms-track submission.** Quantum publishes across theory and NISQ/empirical work; the referee form scores technical correctness *and* reproducibility of experiments as separate items, indicating neither is sufficient alone. For a modular-Shor post-processing contribution, the proven propositions establish correctness/safety (theory reviewers) and the ablation + calibration establish practical value (empirical reviewers) — you must satisfy both. Editors do not read proofs at screening, so the empirical + contributions framing carries the first-decision.

**Structural exemplars in Quantum combining a formal probabilistic model with ablation-style empirical validation:**
- **"Bayesian Quantum Amplitude Estimation" (BAE), Quantum 9, 1856 (2025)** — a problem-tailored, noise-aware Bayesian algorithm with a formal inference model, parallelizable quantum+classical components, a "robust benchmark for amplitude estimation algorithms," and head-to-head empirical comparison; also introduces an annealed variant (aBAE). Strong model for framing an adaptive Bayesian statistic + benchmark.
- **"Harnessing Bayesian Statistics to Accelerate Iterative Quantum Amplitude Estimation" (BIQAE), Quantum 10, 1962 (2026)** — combines "both rigorous mathematical proofs and numerical simulations" to attribute an advantage to Bayesian statistics; an explicit "prove the mechanism + validate numerically" model. This is the closest structural exemplar to your theory+ablation design.
- **"Variational quantum amplitude estimation," Quantum 6, 670 (2022)** — adaptive implementation with empirical cost comparisons vs. classical Monte Carlo.

These three establish that Quantum readily accepts adaptive/Bayesian estimation algorithms that pair a formal probabilistic model with benchmark/ablation validation — directly precedent-setting for the WQPE contribution. The prior arXiv line you build on (2507.22460, "Towards Practical Quantum Phase Estimation: A Modular, Scalable, and Adaptive Approach" — since published in *Advanced Quantum Technologies*, 2026; and 2509.05010, "A Modular, Adaptive, and Scalable Quantum Factoring Algorithm") should be cited as the foundation.

## Details

**Critique checklist for the current compiled report** (apply before drafting):
1. Does a "Summary of Contributions" bullet list appear within the first two pages, each bullet mapping to a proposition/figure? (Quantum editor first-read requirement.)
2. Is every use of "theorem/proposition/lemma" reserved for rigorously proven statements, with heuristics explicitly labeled ("heuristic," "empirically observed," "conjecture")? (The Jaffe–Quinn tradition in mathematically-inclined venues reserves "theorem" for rigorously proven statements and recommends "fact/statement/conjecture" for heuristic ones.)
3. Does every headline empirical claim lead with effect size + CI + regime, with p-value last?
4. Are the McNemar variant, bootstrap B/seed/unit, and Holm family pre-declared and specified?
5. Is there a dedicated main-text Limitations section covering small-N scope, calibration overconfidence, and simulation-only status?
6. Is the audit-bug correction narrated inline (not as "erratum," not buried in a footnote) with appendix quantification and code link?
7. Is code/data released under a FOSS license and referenced? (Positive editorial weight at Quantum; scored reproducibility item.)
8. Are full proofs in a restating appendix with inline sketches only?
9. Does related work end with an explicit positioning paragraph against Bayesian/ML/DP/beam-search/graph-search classes?
10. Does the abstract follow the 5-part structure and state the major contribution early?

**On the calibration honesty check.** The Brier 0.2239→0.1988 (raw → isotonic) result and the decision not to wire recalibration into the default rule is exactly the kind of negative/limitation result reviewers reward disclosure of. Report it in the main text with the reliability-diagram interpretation (raw C_w is overconfident), state why recalibration is not shipped by default (e.g., the conservativity/baseline-reproduction guarantee, or insufficient calibration data), and cross-reference the Limitations section. Isotonic regression is the standard non-parametric recalibration choice, and improving Brier while not being default-wired is a defensible, honestly-scoped design decision.

## Recommendations

**Stage 1 — restructure before rewriting (do first):**
- Add a first-two-pages "Summary of Contributions" bullet list; map each bullet to a section/proposition/figure.
- Move all full proofs to a restating appendix; leave statements + sketches inline. Use `apxproof`/`proof-at-the-end`.
- Move exhaustive OFAT/noise/adversarial sweep tables to appendices; keep representative summaries + the compilation-cliff scaling finding in-text.

**Stage 2 — fix claims and statistics:**
- Relabel every non-proven claim; reserve theorem/proposition/lemma for proven results.
- Rewrite every headline empirical sentence to lead with absolute+relative effect and bootstrap CI, regime, then p.
- Add a "Statistical methods" subsection: McNemar exact-binomial (state discordant counts b,c and the continuity/exactness choice, cite Dietterich 1998), bootstrap B and seed and paired-difference resampling unit, Holm–Bonferroni family definition (cite Holm 1979) with a sorted-p table.

**Stage 3 — integrity and limitations:**
- Write the audit-bug correction inline where the carry-check audit is described; add an appendix before→after + code commit link; no "erratum" label, no apology.
- Write a dedicated main-text Limitations section (small-N scope, calibration overconfidence + non-default recalibration, simulation-only, proven-vs-observed boundary), each paired with a mitigation or future-work note.
- Release code/data under a FOSS license and reference it (scored reproducibility item; positive editorial weight).

**Stage 4 — positioning and target-venue fit:**
- Rewrite related work thematically with a closing positioning paragraph against Bayesian/ML/DP/beam-search/graph-search reconstruction; cite 2507.22460 and 2509.05010 as the foundation.
- Model the theory+ablation structure on BIQAE (Quantum 10, 1962) and BAE (Quantum 9, 1856).
- Prepare for Quantum first; if desk-rejected on "significance beyond state of the art," pivot to ACM TQC (which explicitly welcomes classical-design-methods-for-quantum work), reformatting to the ACM template.

**Benchmarks that would change the plan:** If you can add larger-N or additional composite sizes to the ablation, the "small-N overclaiming" objection weakens and you can make stronger generalization claims. If hardware runs become available, move from "simulation-only" limitation to a hardware-validation contribution. If the family of comparisons is large (>~10) and many survive only BH not Holm, report both and soften claims accordingly. If raw C_w calibration cannot be improved enough to ship, keep recalibration explicitly out of the default rule and frame conservativity as the design priority.

## Caveats
- Quantum states no length limit, but "no length constraint" is not license for a 1000-line body; the binding constraint is that editors read only the first few pages before the (unappealable, most-common) desk decision. Front-loading is more important than absolute length.
- Several writing-craft sources (editing-service blogs, LinkedIn/Medium posts) reflect consensus norms but are not authoritative policy; the authoritative anchors are Quantum's own author/referee/editorial pages, ACM TQC's guidelines, the NeurIPS checklist, COPE/ICMJE, the peer-reviewed ECE/CS statistics playbook, and the primary-source methodology papers (Dietterich 1998; Holm 1979; Altenmüller et al. 2021).
- No exact quantum-domain precedent was found for an inline "headline finding reversed by a self-caught bug" disclosure; the strongest structural exemplars (arXiv:2011.06244, 2210.10266) are software-engineering/IR. The ICMJE/COPE anchors and the "self-correction increases trustworthiness" study support the recommended approach.
- The specific numeric acceptance rate (~50%) comes from a 2019 Quantum author-feedback post and may have drifted (and varies widely by handling editor — one reported 19%); treat as approximate. Quantum's official position remains "no target rejection rate."
- Effect-size and B-value conventions (e.g., B=1,000–10,000) are community norms, not journal mandates; the requirement reviewers enforce is that you *state and justify* your choices and match the resampling unit to the experimental design.
